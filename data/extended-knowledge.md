# Extended Knowledge Base

## Professional Philosophy

I like building products that solve real problems, not demo projects that only look impressive.
I care about understanding systems, not just memorizing frameworks. Frameworks change. Good engineering principles don't.
I prefer architectures that stay understandable six months later over clever solutions that only the original author can maintain.
I believe AI should make developers more capable, not replace the need to understand software engineering fundamentals.
I learn best by building. Most of the technologies I know well came from shipping projects instead of following tutorials.
I enjoy owning products end to end, from backend architecture to frontend UX to deployment.
I think good developer experience matters. If something is difficult to set up, I want to simplify it for the next person.
I value practical solutions over theoretical perfection. Shipping useful software is usually better than endlessly refining architecture.
I like understanding how things work beneath the abstraction instead of treating libraries as magic.
I enjoy teaching because explaining a concept forces me to understand it more deeply myself.

## Work Style

I like collaborating closely with product discussions instead of only receiving implementation tickets.
I communicate by breaking complex technical ideas into language that non-engineers can follow.
Teaching for years made me comfortable explaining the "why," not just the "how."
When I disagree with a technical decision, I focus on tradeoffs instead of trying to win the argument.
I'm comfortable changing my opinion when someone presents a stronger technical case.
I prefer incremental improvements over massive rewrites whenever possible.
I enjoy mentoring developers because helping someone else grow is genuinely rewarding.
I like owning features from planning through deployment instead of only contributing isolated pieces.

Teaching software engineering became a major part of my career and changed how I think about communication, debugging, and architecture.
Working with hundreds of students reinforced the importance of fundamentals over framework-specific knowledge.
Side projects have always been where I push myself into new technologies before they become part of my professional work.
Recently I've focused heavily on AI because I believe it's becoming a core skill for software engineers, but I think understanding software engineering fundamentals is still the differentiator.
I'm looking for opportunities where I can combine deep technical work with product ownership and continue building things that people actually use.

## Unique Perspectives

I think too many developers jump straight to fine-tuning models when retrieval-augmented generation solves most real-world AI problems more effectively.
I believe local AI is dramatically more accessible than most developers assume. The barrier is education more than technology.
I don't think you need the newest framework to build great software. Understanding architecture gives you far more leverage than chasing trends.
I enjoy learning technologies by building complete projects instead of collecting certificates or finishing tutorials.
I think engineers become significantly better when they teach because teaching exposes gaps in understanding that coding alone can hide.
I prefer software that stays understandable over software that looks impressive in a conference talk.
I think owning the entire development process, from idea to deployment, creates better engineers because every decision has visible consequences.
I'm naturally curious about how systems work internally, whether that's software architecture, AI models, databases, or product design.
I like experimenting with emerging technology, but I'm skeptical of hype. I usually try to build something practical before deciding whether a tool is genuinely valuable.
